# For Educators

# Unit Overview

This curricular supplement is designed to provide teachers and students with immediate, local connections to history using stories about a place known throughout time by several names: Tk̓úpa, the Utilla Indian Agency, Fort Henrietta, and the City of Echo, Oregon. The lessons in this unit have been designed to fit the 2024 Oregon Social Science Standards for Grade 4, but could easily be scaled to Grades 3, 6, or 8 if desired.

The content of this unit has been designed in alignment with [Átaw Ititámat (the Educational Values)](https://ctuir.org/departments/education/) outlined by The Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation’s Education Program and with with consideration for the [College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework](https://www.socialstudies.org/standards/c3) for Social Studies State Standards developed by the [National Council for the Social Studies](https://www.socialstudies.org/).

## Essential Questions

How have interactions and relationships between humans, non-human beings, and the environment, changed at the place known as Tk̓úpa in the Sahaptian language (also now known as Echo, Oregon)?  
How have these interactions and relationships remained the same?

## Enduring Understandings

 For thousands of years and continuing into the present, members of the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation (the Cayuse, Umatilla, and Walla Walla tribes) have studied, cared for, honored, been shaped by, and thrived along the Umatilla River. This is particularly true at specific named places.   
\[See also [Essential Understandings of Native Americans in Oregon](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/students-and-family/equity/NativeAmericanEducation/Documents/SB13%20Curriculum/CLEAN%20Essential%20Understandings%20of%20Native%20Americans%20in%20Oregon%20June%202020%20\(002\).pdf)\]

There are many ways to understand and connect with the place or the environment where we live and through which we travel. Maps, place names, and archaeological investigations are different but related ways for people to understand aspects of a place or environment. 

The arrival of various new peoples to a place brings change. These changes may include what places are called, how they are used, and the kinds of relationships people and other beings have there.

Changes brought by newcomers are not linear or universal. Many different peoples’ relationships with the place and some aspects of the place itself remain unchanged.

The study of the past through history and archaeology explores the push and pull of change and continuity throughout time.

## Learning Goals

Throughout this unit, students will…  
… apply terms related to the field of archaeology in their discussions of the past.  
… investigate how larger trends in state and national history directly connect to their local communities.  
… internalize essential understandings regarding Indigenous Peoples in Oregon, particularly the Walla Walla, Cayuse, and Umatilla peoples.

# Lesson Plans

[Lesson 1: Echoes of the Past](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1FFg_FBxrelr3_7XmKbyPI2EzU4x__JzyxYgEH8qJcZ8/edit?usp=drive_link)  
(https://docs.google.com/document/d/1FFg\_FBxrelr3\_7XmKbyPI2EzU4x\_\_JzyxYgEH8qJcZ8/)  
Students examine what they already know about the place currently known as Echo, Oregon, using a timeline activity. Students learn about archaeology and consider how this method of investigating the past can provide new and different information.

[Lesson 2: Tk̓úpa Means Tule Place](https://docs.google.com/document/d/13RiF_bf_wM7ocEXRLIH__EdX9Vp_-VbP185AYxYnxdY/)   
(https://docs.google.com/document/d/13RiF\_bf\_wM7ocEXRLIH\_\_EdX9Vp\_-VbP185AYxYnxdY/)  
Students learn the meaning of the place name Tk̓úpa and explore the relationships among the Indigenous peoples of the area to this particular location along Ímatalam Wána (the Umatilla River). Students compare relationships among Indigenous peoples and newcomers at this place before and during the encroachment of migrants via the Oregon Trail.

[Lesson 3: Territorial Tactics](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1FrnnNu5cKGbkNFGj9OahXjXCALsldRmmrCtNbtFvW0M/)  
(https://docs.google.com/document/d/1FrnnNu5cKGbkNFGj9OahXjXCALsldRmmrCtNbtFvW0M/)  
Students investigate the ways that the Oregon Territorial Government employed different strategies to foster relationships and create or calm conflict with Indigenous peoples to facilitate settler colonialism.

[Lesson 4: A Time of Transition](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1uVJ5CscNcQQsnJjz3LleqCdSkuKjoxQnHyEAbwI61mc/)  
(https://docs.google.com/document/d/1uVJ5CscNcQQsnJjz3LleqCdSkuKjoxQnHyEAbwI61mc/)  
Students explore the economic, agricultural, and social transitions caused by urbanization and the expansion of rail transportation at the turn of the century. Students learn how these changes to the landscape of tk̓úpa still impact daily life today.

[Lesson 5: One Place, Many Meanings](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1WSwr6RibOFcEDI8oLOe3CkhoxX-Vc87Yi_0Qq2eWa4w/)  
(https://docs.google.com/document/d/1WSwr6RibOFcEDI8oLOe3CkhoxX-Vc87Yi\_0Qq2eWa4w/)  
Students review the contents of the unit and demonstrate what they have learned about the place known as Tk̓úpa/Echo through a timeline activity. Students demonstrate understanding of the unit’s enduring understandings through a summative assessment in which they imagine and illustrate a day at Tk̓úpa.

# More Resources

The following resources provide valuable context for this unit as well as opportunities to supplement existing local history curricula:

[Essential Understandings of Native Americans in Oregon](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/students-and-family/equity/NativeAmericanEducation/Documents/SB13%20Curriculum/CLEAN%20Essential%20Understandings%20of%20Native%20Americans%20in%20Oregon%20June%202020%20\(002\).pdf) provides a necessary foundation for all teaching and learning about the Indigenous people of Oregon. This guiding document serves as the basis for all Tribal History / Shared History lessons and should also be applied as appropriate to this unit. 

[Critical Orientations for Indigenous Studies Curriculum](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1YBr7oz17H_YmnemhIWKtifVTvk8g0HND/view) is another guiding document used by the creators of Tribal History / Shared History. It offers educators a framework for orienting their teachings using the 6 Ps: place, presence, perspectives, political nationhood, power, and partnerships. 

The [Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation’s website](https://ctuir.org/about/) provides valuable contextual information for educators including history & culture, First Foods & Life Cycles, and shared language of the Cayuse, Umatilla, and Walla Walla tribes. 

The [CTUIR Impacts to First Foods and Traditional Use Areas](https://www.fws.gov/portlandharbor/sites/portland/files/resources/PortlandHarborNRDATribalFactSheet-%20CTUIR.pdf) provides examples of impacts to First Foods and Traditional use areas at the Portland Harbor Superfund Site, which is a prime example of the impacts throughout homelands in the state of Oregon and beyond.

[Tamástslikt Cultural Institute](https://www.tamastslikt.org/) is the only museum about the Oregon Trail that tells the story of western expansion from tribal points of view. Tamástslikt celebrates the customs and practices of the Cayuse, Umatilla, and Walla Walla Tribes through interactive displays, special events, and informative programming. Admission is free for school groups with a reservation and its [Teacher Resources Page](https://www.tamastslikt.org/explore/#edu) provides specific lessons developed by the tribe. 

The [Confluence Project](https://www.confluenceproject.org/) seeks to connect people to the history, living cultures, and ecology of the Columbia River system through indigenous voices. Its [virtual library](https://www.confluenceproject.org/library/) includes many excellent video clips and podcast episodes which illustrate diverse perspectives. This is an excellent way to bring “guest speakers” into your classroom without needing to deal with scheduling challenges. The Confluence Project can also help arrange in-person Indigenous educators to come to your classroom.

The [Oregon Trail Curriculum Project](https://blogs.uoregon.edu/otcurriculumproject/) provides ready-to-use lesson plans and teaching resources designed to supplement oversimplified curricula about the Oregon Trail and support responsible teaching and learning about the Oregon Trail.

This article from the Confluence Project provides guidance for teachers on [How to Successfully Welcome a Native Educator into Your Classroom](https://www.confluenceproject.org/library-post/how-to-successfully-welcome-a-native-educator-into-your-classroom/).
